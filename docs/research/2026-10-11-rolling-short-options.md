# Make assignment the default, rolling the exception

For this wheel, rolling should be a narrow exception path, not the system's main way of managing positions. The evidence supports one kind of roll: a **challenged short rolled out in time at the same or a more favourable strike for a net credit, once, with the same contract count**. It supports nothing broader. Paying a debit to roll, rolling on a timer, rolling unchallenged shorts, and rolling repeatedly have no supporting evidence, and some of them have evidence against. Assignment is the wheel's built-in repair. By put-call parity, a same-strike put roll and "take assignment, then sell a same-strike call" are nearly the same position. So the real choices are what the next strike should be and what each path costs, not whether to "save" the trade. The current implementation is well guarded at the order boundary but cannot produce a trade: **54 roll alerts, 0 roll candidates, 0 orders**. Four independent structural defects each block it on their own. It also encodes the wrong policy. It runs defensive rolls on routine 21-DTE and 7-DTE timers (47 of the 54 alerts). It demands a 0.10 delta cut that rules out the evidence-favoured same-strike roll. It has no assignment branch, no cost-basis rule and no roll limits. Its generator allows a $0.50 debit that its executor then rejects. The redesign below has four parts. Fix the plumbing first: fetch the exact contract, use one shared roll policy, show the real candidate on the card, and gate on RTH with retry. Then replace the timers with a strike-breach trigger feeding a deterministic decision between assign, let called away, roll and close. Then cap the sequence at one roll by default. Every threshold stays in config. Most thresholds are practitioner consensus or inference, because the quantified evidence is a handful of tastylive SPY studies seen only as search snippets.

## The evidence supports one narrow roll: same strike, later expiry, net credit

The quantified evidence on rolling is thin and comes from one vendor. Every tastylive study page the researchers fetched redirected to a show index, so the figures below come from search-engine snippets, not results tables. The underlying is SPY, the trades are mostly 45-DTE positions at 16–30 delta, and no costs are reported. Within those limits the studies point one way. In a 2015 strangle study, rolling the tested side out to the next monthly expiration **at the same strike "increased profitability by 173%" and win rate by 17%**. Rolling further OTM required a debit and **"decreased our win rate by 17%"**. The summary concluded that paying a debit to roll is "never a good idea" ([tastylive Research Recap](https://www.tastylive.com/shows/research-recap/episodes/rolling-tested-strikes-in-3-mins-or-less-10-21-2015)). tastylive's own summary article adds that rolling at a **strike breach** beats rolling at a breakeven breach on credit and win rate, and that same-strike rolls are "the more optimal choice, on average, over time" ([tastylive](https://tastylive.com/news-insights/why-traders-roll-positions)). The 173% is a relative figure on an unstated base, from a two-sided strangle, on the index whose puts are most overpriced. In tastylive's own data, **16-delta SPY puts finished ITM 5% of the time against 16% implied** ([tastytrade India 2020](https://s3.amazonaws.com/tastytradepublicmedia/website/cms/tastytrade_TomSosnoff_India2020.pdf/original/tastytrade_TomSosnoff_India2020.pdf)). That mean reversion is what makes "roll and wait" look good, and it is the part least likely to carry over to single stocks and leveraged ETFs.

Two further findings argue against a hair-trigger. First, being tested is a weak predictor of loss on an index. In tastylive's SPY study, 16-delta puts were tested 14% of the time, yet **53% of tested puts still expired profitable**, for an average P&L of only **−$40 per contract** ([tastylive Market Measures](https://www.tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/rolling-puts-12-07-2016)). That study's own design held tested puts while more than four weeks remained and rolled only once. Second, loss rules reshape the distribution and usually lower the mean. A 2× stop on strangles cut the worst loss from **−$1,325 to −$340** but generally underperformed holding, and in high IV, holding won outright ([tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/exiting-losing-trades-12-16-2014); [tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/news-insights/Do-Smaller-Losses-Lead-to-Better-Performance)). A 3× stop came closest to early management ([tastylive](https://tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/should-i-ever-use-a-stop-loss-05-09-2024)). Euan Sinclair, as quoted by Kris Abdelmessih, puts it bluntly: "the expected value of a strategy will fall if you use a stop". Abdelmessih argues for exits based on risk limits ("Is the risk too big or not?") rather than rules triggered by P&L ([Moontower](https://moontowermeta.com/stop-with-the-stop-loss-debate/)). A roll is economically a stop on the old leg plus a new trade. A practitioner who tested the tasty mechanics found a ~70% win rate with **negative ROI**, and notes there is "no promise that your option will go back OTM if you keep rolling it" ([Sweet Volatility](https://sweetvolatility.com/tasty-trade-experiments/)).

The wheel already has its own repair mechanism. In tastylive's 30-delta SPY put study, puts won 92% of the time. For the 8% that were assigned, **selling a 30-delta call turned 45% of the losers profitable** ([tastylive](https://tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/short-puts-converting-to-covered-calls-11-08-2016)). Theory explains why this rivals rolling. Put-call parity, the identity Schwab uses to read a call's extrinsic value off the same-strike put ([Schwab](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/ex-dividend-dates-understanding-dividend-risk)), makes long stock plus a short call at strike K worth the same as a short put at K plus cash. **A same-strike put roll and "take assignment, then sell a same-strike call to the same expiry" are close to the same position.** They differ in dividends, early exercise and transaction costs, and in which strike the wheel's covered-call rules would actually choose. No study compares the two head to head, and none of the sources quantifies the cost of either path. The parity argument still settles what the decision is about: where the next strike sits and what the transition costs, not "saving" a trade. spintwig's finding that the stock leg explained **94–99% of total SPY wheel return**, with option exit rules "inconsequential", points the same way ([spintwig](https://spintwig.com/spy-wheel-45-dte-options-backtest)).

On the covered-call side, broker education and practitioners agree. A deep-ITM call is mostly intrinsic value, so "any roll will likely require a debit and won't improve your upside much". Assignment "is not a failure—it's a completed trade" ([Saxo](https://www.home.saxo/en-sg/content/articles/options/rolling-options-explained---04---frequently-asked-questions-and-real-world-scenarios-02102025)). Rolling has more appeal only when the call's strike is below cost basis, where assignment would realise a loss ([SpotGamma](https://spotgamma.com/covered-calls-below-cost-basis/)). On ex-dividend risk the evidence is firm and theoretical. Early exercise of an American call is rational only just before an ex-date, and only when the dividend exceeds the call's remaining time value ([FIU lecture notes](https://faculty.fiu.edu/~dupoyetb/Options_seminar/lectures/lecture9.pdf)). Schwab's working rule is that a call whose extrinsic value is below the dividend on the day before the ex-date is likely to be assigned ([Schwab](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/ex-dividend-dates-understanding-dividend-risk)). Short puts face early assignment when they are deep ITM with little time value left ([Schwab](https://international.schwab.com/story/risks-options-assignment)).

Three areas have no usable evidence at all: leveraged ETFs, high-IV single names and earnings. Nothing rigorous covers rolling on TQQQ- or SOXL-type products. Volatility decay means a tested short put on one faces negative drift in choppy markets ([Valuelytica](https://valuelytica.substack.com/p/vdc-qqq)), so the "tested puts recover" base rate is presumably lower there, but nobody has measured it. The same holds for rolling around earnings and for delta-threshold triggers. ORATS's backtester can exit on a leg's delta, but no published results exist ([ORATS](https://orats.com/blog/how-to-backtest-exiting-a-spread-when-one-of-the-legs-goes-in-the-money)). Wheel guides converge on "**roll once, maximum twice, then take assignment or exit**" and on never rolling for a net debit ([QuantWheel](https://quantwheel.com/learn/rolling-options); [Wheel Strategy Substack](https://wheelstrategy.substack.com/p/how-to-roll-options-wheel-strategy)). That is consensus, not data.

## Build the new leg as if it were a fresh trade, with credit as a guard-rail

The construction literature is entirely practitioner and broker education. Its organising principle is that a roll is "simply two trades" ([Saxo](https://www.home.saxo/en-mena/content/articles/options/rolling-options-explained---01---understanding-the-basics-and-why-rolling-matters-12092025)). You roll "only if our assumption is still the same" ([tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/definitions/rolling-options)), and only "if you'd genuinely open that new position fresh today", because "rolling can't fix a deteriorating thesis, it can only buy time against one" ([QuantWheel](https://quantwheel.com/learn/rolling-options-explained/)). The net-credit rule fits under that principle as a guard against paying to postpone a loss. It is not an expected-value test. tastylive rolls "for duration and a small credit" and does "not double our risk by doubling our contracts" ([tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/definitions/rolling-options)). Option Alpha accepts "at least a credit … or if nothing else, a very small debit", and otherwise prefers to take the loss ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/lessons/mechanics-of-rolling)). Any later-dated option has more time value, so a credit is easy to manufacture just by adding duration. That is why the credit rule needs an extension cap beside it.

There is one real conflict to resolve before a credit rule can be encoded. The "fresh trade" test, read literally, would require the new leg to pass the entry delta band of 0.20–0.35. A defensive roll of a breached short almost never can. The same-strike roll the evidence favours lands at or near 0.50 delta. Parity resolves the conflict. An ITM short put is mostly synthetic long stock, so "would I open this fresh?" means "would I hold this stock exposure for N more days?". That is a test on the underlying (still eligible, would-own, liquid, not into earnings, inside concentration limits), not on the option's delta. The defensible delta constraint is that **the new leg's delta must not exceed the old leg's**. A minimum delta reduction is not defensible.

The tastylive extrinsic-value finding explains why same-strike and modestly ITM rolls work and deep-ITM rolls do not. The extrinsic gap between two expiries "is greatest at the ATM strike" ([tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/news-insights/three-ways-to-trade-extrinsic-value-at-different-strikes)). Ally puts the practical limit at roughly **2–4% ITM**. Beyond that, "it will be tough to roll for an acceptable net debit, never mind receiving a net credit" ([OptionsPlaybook](https://www.optionsplaybook.com/managing-positions/rolling-cash-secured-puts)). A credit floor therefore makes deep-ITM rolls drop out naturally, which is the outcome the evidence wants: those positions should be assigned, called away or closed. Ally's worked example shows the bookkeeping trap the system already falls into. A $50 put rolled to a 90-day $47.50 put for **$0.15 net credit** books a loss on the front-month buyback. The back-month premium "is not secured gain" ([OptionsPlaybook](https://www.optionsplaybook.com/managing-positions/rolling-cash-secured-puts)). After a roll, P&L and exit lines have to be measured on the whole chain, not on the new leg's gross premium. Fidelity's formulas (new breakeven = old breakeven ∓ net credit or debit) give the chain-level numbers the approval card should show ([Fidelity](https://www.fidelity.com/learning-center/investment-products/options/rolling-covered-calls)).

## Fifty-four alerts, zero rolls: the current design cannot reach a trade

The audit's central finding, checked against `HEAD` for this report, is that **every one of the 54 stored roll alerts (2026-05-30 to 2026-10-09) went out `approvable=False`**. The database has no roll candidates, approvals, orders or fills. The production log has no "queued candidate" or "combo placed" lines; those strings appear only in pytest logs. By trigger, the alerts break down as **manage_dte 36, dte 11, loss_multiple 5, delta_drift 2**. No `strike_breach`, `assignment_risk`, `iv_spike` or `ex_div` alert has ever fired. Measured against the evidence above, 47 of the 54 alerts are timers that should never have asked for a roll at all. The five loss-line alerts came at the 09:30 ET opening print on shorts at |Δ| 0.19–0.36, mostly far OTM: the "tested but likely to recover" case where the evidence says hold. The record is not evidence that the system missed needed rolls. It mostly shows noise triggers meeting a generator that could not have answered them anyway.

Four defects each independently prevent a roll. First, the chain fetcher builds only out-of-the-money contracts against the prior close (`src/ibkr/market_data.py:790-810`, confirmed) inside the 7–28 DTE window (`:728-736`). An ITM short or a short at ≤ 6 DTE is therefore missing from `quotes`, `current_mid` is `None`, and every candidate is skipped (`src/strategies/rolling.py:111-112`). That is exactly the situation the escalation triggers exist for. Second, the defensive rule requires `|old Δ| − |new Δ| ≥ 0.10` with the new leg inside the 0.20–0.35 entry band (`rolling.py:62-78, 115-132`). **Any short below |Δ| 0.30 can therefore never roll**, and a same-strike roll, the one the evidence favours, is excluded by construction. Third, the generator allows up to a $0.50 debit, but `execute_roll` rejects any `live_net_credit <= 0` as `roll_net_debit` (`src/execution/roll_executor.py:328-337`, confirmed), and a test pins that behaviour (`tests/test_roll_executor.py:312-332`). Every debit roll the operator approves is therefore rejected after the tap. Fourth, the card with the Approve button is rendered by `format_roll_alert(pos, quote, fresh, review)`, which never receives the candidate (`src/monitor/intraday.py:428`, `src/notify/formatters.py:1925-1974`). The operator would approve a strike, expiry and net price they cannot see, next to a Claude-invented `roll_target` that need not match.

Three more defects would bite as soon as rolls began to flow. `_resolve_short_to_close` picks the nearest-dated short on the same underlying and right, not the alerted contract, and takes its size from that short (`roll_executor.py:70-93, 351`, confirmed). GOOGL 330P 10/09 and 335P 10/16 were open together on 2026-10-02, which is exactly this case. The one-shot `dte`/`manage_dte` alerts fire with no RTH gate and are never retried. They were stored at 01:00 ET and 03:25 ET, when the AMZN fetch returned 36 quotes against a dead book (`intraday.py:238-279, 401-408`). The roll fetch is the full scan fetch, `get_option_chain_quotes_async` over every expiry and strike in the window (`src/execution/roll_pipeline.py:43-69`, confirmed). That is the same 11–40 s warm and ~125 s cold cost as a universe symbol, issued in `chain_batch_size` (40-line) batches (`market_data.py:1118-1127`). It comes from the monitor process, whose share of the 90-line budget is `reserved_monitor_lines: 20` (`src/common/config.py:1188-1196`). This review did not measure whether those 40 lines can coincide with the scanner's 40 and the spreads book's 30. The budget arithmetic does not account for them either way.

On policy, the comparison with the evidence is unflattering but not one-sided.

The guardrails at the order boundary are sound and should survive the redesign unchanged. The Claude reviewer only writes text, approval is a human tap, both legs are re-qualified and freshly quoted at send time, and the combo cannot leave the wheel half-legged. One thing has never been checked: the BAG sign convention has been verified for spread verticals on paper, but never for a roll.

## A deterministic roll policy the Rules Engine can own

The redesign treats every roll evaluation as two separable decisions, following Sinclair and Abdelmessih. The first is an exit decision on the old leg, driven by risk and by whether the system still wants the exposure. The second is whether to open the new leg, which must pass the same underlying-level gates as a fresh trade. A new module, `src/strategies/roll_policy.py`, returns a `RollDecision` (assign / let called away / roll / close / hold / defer, plus a reason code). It is pure deterministic Python fed only by schemas. Any resulting order, whether a roll or a proposed close, goes through `validate_candidates` before the card is sent and again at send time. No LLM output enters the decision. The Claude review receives the finished candidate and alternatives and can annotate them, nothing more. Grades below: **E** = evidence-backed (study, exchange rule or theory, however thin), **C** = practitioner consensus, **I** = inference or judgement.

### Triggers: breach and dividend, not the calendar

| Trigger | Rule | Action | Grade |
|---|---|---|---|
| Strike breach | Spot through the strike, or |Δ| ≥ `roll.trigger.breach_delta` (0.50), and DTE ≤ `roll.trigger.evaluate_within_dte` (10), or extrinsic ≤ `roll.trigger.min_extrinsic` ($0.10) | Run the decision tree | E (breach > breakeven; tastylive held trades with > 4 weeks left); thresholds I |
| Ex-dividend, short call | Ex-date ≤ expiry, ex-date within `roll.ex_div.days_ahead` trading days, and extrinsic (same-strike put mid as proxy) < dividend + `roll.ex_div.buffer` | Run the call branch; must resolve by the session before the ex-date | E (theory, Schwab); needs a dividend-amount source the notes did not confirm exists |
| Loss line, non-leveraged | Chain cost-to-close ≥ `max_loss_multiple` × chain net credit | Alert only, unless the breach trigger also holds | E (stops lower the mean) / I |
| Leveraged ETF loss line | Existing 2× auto-buyback (`profit_take.py:270-331`) | Unchanged; never roll | I (risk-limit rule on decaying products) |
| `manage_dte`, `dte`, `iv_spike`, `delta_drift`, `assignment_risk` | Keep as informational alerts, or fold into breach | No roll request | E (no roll evidence); `assignment_risk` 0.70 is subsumed by 0.50 |
| Timing | RTH only, ≥ `roll.trigger.min_minutes_after_open` (15) after the open, re-evaluated every `roll.trigger.reevaluate_minutes` (30) while the trigger holds, not one-shot | Defer outside the window | I (opening spreads inflated all five loss alerts) |

Retiring the timers as roll triggers is the single biggest policy change. The prior profit-taking research reached the matching conclusion: a 7–10 DTE book is inside the gamma window from entry, so a time stop belongs in the profit-take schedule, not in a defensive roll. An "income roll" of an unchallenged winner forward in the same name is also dominated by the existing loop of a 50% close followed by a fresh universe-wide scan, which can put the freed collateral into the best candidate anywhere. The dead `defensive=False` branch should be deleted rather than revived (I).

The default at step 4 is the main judgement call in the design, so its reasoning should be explicit. Parity makes the same-strike roll and assignment-plus-call economically close. Assignment keeps the wheel's own covered-call rules (strike ≥ basis, delta band) in charge of the next strike. It needs no new-leg selection logic and spends nothing on a two-leg combo spread. The evidence for rolling over holding comes from strangles on an overpriced-put index, which is not the case here. Under this default, rolling becomes what the redesign intends: the path for positions the system cannot or should not take delivery of, plus covered calls the operator explicitly wants to keep. An operator who trusts the tastylive result can flip one key.

### Candidate construction and the targeted fetch

Construction runs on a **targeted fetch**, not the scan fetch. It requests the alerted contract by conId, plus the next `roll.construct.max_expiries` (3) expiries after the current one up to `roll.construct.target_dte_max` (35). Within those it covers `roll.construct.strikes_outward` (5) listed strikes from the current strike away from the money. That is about 15–20 contracts, which fit in a single batch capped at `roll.construct.max_quote_lines` (10–20, sized to the monitor's reservation minus its standing lines), against up to 320 contracts and 40-line batches today. With the contract cache warm it should take seconds rather than the 11–130 s of the full fetch (I, not measured). It needs no new line budget, which matters with 40 + 30 + 20 = 90 fully allocated.

The selection rule is lexicographic. For each expiry from nearest to furthest, the system finds the most favourable strike (lowest put, highest call) that meets every constraint and stops at the first expiry that yields one. The constraints are: net credit at mid ≥ `roll.construct.min_net_credit` ($0.05/share, enough to cover two legs at an assumed $0.65/contract with some slippage; I); new |Δ| ≤ old |Δ|; contracts equal to the alerted position; liquidity gates pass on both legs; the new expiry is before the next earnings date; for calls, the strike is ≥ adjusted basis (or ≥ the old strike if already below basis) and the ex-dividend extrinsic test passes; and the extension stays within budget. Debits are possible only on the call branch with `roll.cc.keep_shares: true`, capped by `roll.cc.max_debit`. Every rejection writes a reason code (`no_credit_at_any_strike`, `earnings_before_expiry`, `extension_cap`, …), so the next 54 alerts explain themselves instead of failing silently in a `continue`.

### Gating, limits and execution

A roll candidate passes `validate_candidates` **before** the card is sent. It is then queue-re-gated after the tap and re-gated at send time, all three under one shared `RollPolicy` object. That makes the generator and the executor contradiction impossible by construction. Rolls stay exempt from the ROC, annualised-yield and VRP floors (rolls book no ROC). They should also be exempt from the IV-rank entry floor, which is an entry-selection filter, not a risk limit (I). The earnings check for rolls is "the new expiry precedes earnings" without the 14-day proximity clause. Each roll carries a chain identity: `roll_chain_id`, `roll_seq`, `rolled_from_con_id`, cumulative net credit and the original expiry. `max_rolls_per_chain` defaults to **1**, matching tastylive's single-roll design and the low end of the "once, maximum twice" consensus. `max_extension_days` defaults to 28 beyond the original expiry (I). After a roll, the 50% profit-take and the loss line measure **chain** P&L against chain net credit, not the new leg's inflated gross premium (I, following Ally's "not secured gain"). Roll fills attach to campaigns, so reporting can show the chain.

Execution keeps the BAG combo `LimitOrder` and the LIVE double-confirm. It resolves the short by the stored `rolled_from_con_id` and refuses if that position's size has changed since approval. Pricing starts at the combo mid and walks toward the natural price in `roll.exec.reprice_steps` (3) bounded steps. It never crosses the credit floor or the debit cap, and it stops at `fill_timeout_minutes` (I; today's repricer chases mid only). The first approved roll on paper is the BAG-sign verification test and should be watched by hand.

Live data will validate the mechanics, meaning fills, combo sign and reason codes, long before it can validate the policy. The policy has to rest on the offline backtest and on reading the reason-coded decisions.

## Conclusion

The research changes the question. "When should the system roll?" turns out to be mostly "when should it decline to take delivery?", because parity makes rolling and assignment-then-calls near-substitutes, and the only quantified case for rolling comes from an index whose puts are systematically overpriced. Seen that way, the system's zero executed rolls are less of a loss than they look. Most of what it tried to roll were timer events that the evidence says to leave alone. What it lacked was the logic that decides between assignment, called-away shares and a close, so every challenged position defaulted to whatever happened at expiry, without a recorded reason. A small policy can carry most of the value: breach-triggered, credit-only, same-or-better strike, one roll, then hand off to the wheel. The single non-obvious engineering lesson is that the targeted conId fetch, needed first to make rolls work at all, also turns the expensive part of rolling (a universe-sized chain fetch on a line budget with zero headroom) into a few seconds on a handful of lines.

The remaining uncertainty is real and should stay visible in config comments. Every numeric threshold above, from 0.50 breach delta and $0.05 floor to one roll and 28 days, is consensus or judgement layered on snippet-level vendor evidence. The leveraged-ETF and single-stock behaviour of tested shorts has never been measured by anyone. The system is in a position to measure it, read-only and behind the eval fence, once rolls can actually be evaluated and every evaluation leaves a reason behind.
