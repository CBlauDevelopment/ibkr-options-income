# Tune profit targets by cost, not DTE

For this wheel the right profit-take threshold depends mostly on **what a close costs relative to the credit**, not on entry DTE. The hypothesis behind the question, "60–80% for longer-dated entries, 50% for short-dated", is half-supported. A rising threshold for longer DTE holds up only as a risk-shaping rule: book the profit around the time ~21 DTE remain. It does not raise expected profit. Short-dated entries, which are almost all of this system's book (16 of 21 entries were 7–10 DTE), lean toward a *higher* target than 50%, not a lower one, because a close costs a larger share of a small premium. In the research team's own Monte Carlo model, any take-profit level from 25% to 90% earns about the same with zero costs. Once realistic costs are added, 70–80% beats 50% at every DTE tested, while holding to expiry is clearly worse at 45–90 DTE. Moving entries to 45–60+ DTE is a poor trade for this system. The academic evidence puts the richest premium per day in the front month. Here, a longer window would block roughly half of single-stock entries through the earnings blackout, lose the scoring dedupe to front weeklies, and double or triple chain-fetch time with no line headroom left to absorb it. The system's own record of 12 closed paper trades cannot settle any of this. The first moves are to label why each position closed, measure close costs by DTE, and run a cost-aware backtest sweep. The threshold change itself is cheap and low-stakes, and it should come after those three.

## The threshold level barely moves expected profit

The intuition that "theta accelerates into expiry, so hold for the fast part" is an at-the-money fact, and this system does not sell at the money. In the research team's Black-Scholes model (25-delta put, IV 30%, spot pinned; **model output, not a cited source**), an OTM put decays almost linearly. It reaches **50% profit at about 46% of its life and 80% at about 71%**, and that shape is nearly identical at 30, 45, 60 and 90 DTE. An ATM option needs 75% of its life to reach 50%. Textbook sources agree on the direction: OTM decay is slower and flatter until near expiry, while ATM decay accelerates ([Schwab](https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/theta-decay-options-trading); [CME Group](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/courses/option-greeks/theta.hideSubnav.educationIframe.html)). So entry DTE changes how many calendar days a threshold takes, not what fraction of the trade's life it consumes. Any principled DTE-dependent rule has to come from somewhere other than the decay curve.

The team's Monte Carlo makes the stronger point. With implied vol equal to realised vol (no variance risk premium), **every exit rule had zero expected value**, as the optional-stopping theorem predicts. Profit targets only reshaped the distribution, raising win rate from about 77–81% to 86–92% and fattening the loss per losing trade. With a VRP added (realised vol 85% of implied), take-profit levels from 25% to 90% stayed within about ±0.2 percentage points a year of each other at zero cost. Seed noise was ±0.5 pp. Holding to expiry was **15–25% worse** at every DTE. The reason is conditional. The paths that hit a target are mostly ones where the stock rallied, which leaves an option with low dollar gamma, so it harvests the VRP more slowly than a fresh 25-delta position would. All of these figures are model outputs. They assume constant IV, no skew, no assignment, and immediate redeployment into an equally good trade.

Costs are what make the level matter. Table 1 shows the team's model ROC (annualised on strike collateral, pure option edge) under three cost assumptions. c0 is no cost. c1 is a $0.65 commission plus a 3¢ half-spread, about 2.7% of the credit per side at 30 DTE. c2 is $0.65 plus a 10¢ half-spread.

| Entry DTE | TP 50% (c0 / c1 / c2) | TP 75–80% (c0 / c1 / c2) | Hold to expiry (c0 / c1 / c2) |
|---|---|---|---|
| 30 | 6.40 / 4.39 / 0.55 | 6.34 / 4.98 / 2.35 (75%) | 5.12 / 4.66 / 3.76 |
| 45 | 4.81 / 3.43 / 0.79 | 5.06 / 4.12 / 2.32 (75%) | 4.07 / 3.75 / 3.14 |
| 60 | 4.49 / 3.43 / 1.39 | 4.62 / 3.95 / 2.67 (80%) | 3.72 / 3.49 / 3.03 |
| 90 | 3.31 / 2.59 / 1.21 | 3.51 / 3.07 / 2.20 (80%) | 2.95 / 2.79 / 2.48 |

*Table 1. Research team's Monte Carlo (60,000 paths per cell, GBM, RV/IV = 0.85). Model output, not a cited source.*

At realistic cost, 75–80% beats 50% by roughly 15–20% at every DTE. At wide spreads, holding wins outright. The cost effect has no strong DTE gradient in relative terms, but its absolute bite is largest where the premium is smallest. In the team's arithmetic, closing a 30-DTE put at 80% spends about **14% of the remaining premium** on friction at c1 and about 40% at c2 (model). The empirical record agrees on the mechanism. spintwig charges $1.00 to close and $0.00 to expire worthless. In its SPY wheel, the "50% or 21 DTE" rule spent **4.26% of profit on commission against 0.43%** for holding at 5-delta ([spintwig](https://spintwig.com/spy-wheel-45-dte-options-backtest)). Early Retirement Now reported that 50% management on SPY 5-delta weeklies lowered returns, partly through commission drag ([ERN Part 5](https://earlyretirementnow.com/2020/06/17/passive-income-through-option-writing-part-5); search summary, page not directly fetched).

Profit targets also do almost nothing to the tail. In the model, per-trade 5% CVaR sat at about **−5 to −5.7× the credit for every take-profit rule**, because without a stop-loss the losers are the paths that never reached any target (model output). Raising the threshold is therefore not a risk decision for an individual trade. It changes the mix of position ages in the book, and the book's tail risk per unit of time is set by delta and sizing.

## Both camps agree managing matters more as DTE lengthens

The empirical and theory notes look like they point in opposite directions. Read closely, they answer different questions, and the genuine disagreement is narrow.

### Where they agree

The best empirical source on DTE, Theta Desk's 20-year SPX model of 16-delta puts at 5, 10, 21, 30 and 45 DTE, finds that **"the benefit of managing grows with DTE."** The 50% rule fires on 79% of 5-DTE trades but 96% of 45-DTE trades. Its capital-velocity edge is "real but small" at 5 DTE and nearly doubles by 45. At 45 DTE, managing cut the average lockup from 46 to 15 days and lifted annualised velocity from 2.4% to 4.4%, while holding earned more per trade (0.29% vs 0.19% of collateral) ([Theta Desk](https://thetadesk.substack.com/p/should-you-close-winners-at-50-i)). This is a **manage-versus-hold** comparison at a single 50% threshold. It uses VIX-modelled prices and no costs. It does not compare 50% with 75%. The theory note agrees on exactly this point: at 45–90 DTE every take-profit rule beats holding, and at 30 DTE the gap is smaller and reverses under wide spreads (Table 1). tastylive's 45-DTE SPY put study found managing at 25%, 50% or 21 DTE all ended "very close" and all beat holding ([tastylive 2018](https://www.tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/short-put-management-performance-09-12-2018); episode removed, via search snippet). Having an exit at long DTE matters more than its exact level. Neither camp disputes that.

Both camps also lean higher at short DTE. Theta Desk's small velocity gain at 5 DTE, the theory's cost channel, and practitioner targets of 70% on 1–4 DTE SPX puts ([WealthyOption](https://wealthyoption.com/)) and 85% in Option Alpha's 0DTE bot template ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/bots/0dte-research-insights-bot)) all point the same way. So does the near-equal gross Sharpe of Cboe's hold-to-expiry weekly put index ([Indexology](https://www.indexologyblog.com/2016/02/18/paper-by-professor-bondarenko-has-intriguing-new-analysis-of-put-and-wput-indexes/)). The one contradiction is Option Alpha's TLT strangle test. There, managing 10-DTE trades at 50% doubled annual return (0.66% to 1.37%) and roughly halved drawdown compared with holding ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/podcast/when-to-close-option-position)). That is a single ETF with 30-delta two-sided positions, and it tested 50% only against holding, not against 75%.

### Where they genuinely disagree

The real conflict is over what to do with a long-dated winner that hits 50% early. The empirical camp says close it. tastylive's "45 DTE, manage at 50% or 21 DTE" is the standard, and the empirical note found **no study supporting a higher threshold for longer DTE**. The theory note derives a rising schedule from residual gamma. If the goal is to book profit before ~21 DTE remain (tastylive's gamma zone), and a 25-delta put reaches X% at a nearly fixed fraction of its life, the highest admissible threshold is about **55–58% at 45 DTE, ~70% at 60 DTE and 82–85% at 90 DTE**. At 30 DTE even 50% lands with ~16 DTE left, inside the zone (model derivation). This is internally consistent with tastylive's own pair: "50% at 45 DTE" and "21 DTE" coincide precisely because OTM decay is near-linear.

Three facts dissolve most of the conflict. First, the empirical "no support" is an absence of tests, not evidence against. The only cells that compared a higher target with 50% leaned toward the higher target. On 30-DTE SPY put spreads, a 75% target (with a 15-DTE exit and 25% stop) beat the same rule at 50% (Sharpe 0.83 vs 0.77) and holding (0.62) ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/blog/spy-put-credit-spread-backtest)). At 60 DTE on TLT, 75% beat holding, but 50% was never run ([Option Alpha](https://optionalpha.com/podcast/when-to-close-option-position)). A third-party summary of spintwig's withdrawn studies put the best take-profit "between the 75% and 50%" at 45 DTE ([7 Circles](https://the7circles.uk/options-11-spintwig-efficiency/)). Second, the theory's rising schedule is explicitly **risk-shaping, not EV-maximising**. It lowers path variance, while EV stays flat in X. Third, the time stop does the risk work, not the threshold. In the model, adding a 21-DTE stop to a 45-DTE, 70%-target rule improved per-trade CVaR from −552% to −329% of the credit but cut c1 ROC from 4.07% to 2.88% (model output). Theta Desk likewise found that "50% or 21 DTE" lowered velocity relative to plain 50%. The author notes that a close-only weekly test understates the rule's gamma benefit ([Theta Desk](https://thetadesk.substack.com/p/should-you-close-winners-at-50-i)).

The reconciled position for long-dated entries is a two-part rule. Use a generous profit target, about 75%, to limit round trips, plus a time trigger that closes anything at or above ~50% once ~21 DTE remain. On average this books profit in the same residual-DTE window as tastylive's 50%/21 rule, pays fewer closing costs, and keeps the gamma control that both camps value. Which of "close at 50% early" and "let it run to 75%" wins in practice depends on two quantities this system can measure but has not. One is the round-trip close cost relative to the credit. The other is whether freed collateral is actually redeployed at once into an equally good trade. The case for early closing rests entirely on that second quantity ([ERN](https://earlyretirementnow.com/2020/06/17/passive-income-through-option-writing-part-5) makes the same point).

## The 7–10 DTE book sits where higher targets pay

The live system applies one scalar, `scheduler.profit_take_pct: 50`, to every short option, CSPs and CCs alike, every 15-minute RTH cycle (`src/execution/profit_take.py:211`, `:253`). It ignores DTE and time elapsed, and it auto-closes at mid through a path that deliberately bypasses the Rules Engine because a close reduces risk (`src/execution/position_manager.py:20-24`). The record shows how it actually behaves: **11 profit-takes captured 50–58% after 1–5 days on entries of 7–16 DTE**. At those holding periods, 50% arrives through a quick favourable move, not through theta. Under the theory's conditioning argument, that is the case where closing and re-centring earns its keep, *if* a replacement trade is available. The model's "glide" rule (take profit when profit ≥ 50% + 40% × fraction of life elapsed) captures this. It was the best or near-best rule at c0 and c1 across DTEs (e.g. 45 DTE: 5.62 / 4.51 vs TP50 5.32 / 3.94; model output), and the system's actual 50–58% captures in the first few days already resemble its early-life behaviour.

For the bulk of the book, the defensible change is a modest increase for CSPs, not a cut. A 7-DTE put taken at 75% closes with about 2 DTE left. In the model its per-trade tail is unchanged, its loss frequency rises from about 11% to 16%, and its net rate improves wherever per-side cost is at least ~2% of the credit (model). Whether this system's names clear that bar is unmeasured. The GOOGL 335P entered at a $2.98 credit implies about 1–2% per side for a 3–5¢ half-spread (illustrative arithmetic). Lower-premium contracts on leveraged ETFs plausibly sit well above that. In a wheel, the extra assignments that come from holding longer are not losses in themselves on would-own names. They move exposure into the stock leg, and spintwig found that leg explained **94–99% of total wheel return**, with option exit rules "inconsequential" ([spintwig](https://spintwig.com/spy-wheel-45-dte-options-backtest)). That finding also sets expectations. Threshold tuning is a second-order lever next to delta, strike and which names the system ends up owning.

Covered calls deserve their own, higher setting. The short call's "profit" mostly arrives when the stock falls or stalls, and re-selling after a quick 50% on a falling stock locks in a lower cap. ORATS's 16-mega-cap covered-call study found **"holding to expiration was better than exiting after profit"** against a 75% exit, with 30 DTE and 10-delta best ([ORATS](https://orats.com/blog/covered-call-backtest-finding-the-best-maturity-strike-iv-and-earnings-methods)). ORATS published no numbers and stated no costs, so this is directional only. A CC threshold of 75–80%, or hold with an ex-dividend guard, is better supported than 50%. Note that `events.ex_dividend_assignment_guard: true` is not read by any code today. Ex-dividend exists only as a monitor alert within 5 days and a fair-value anchor (`src/monitor/triggers.py:187-200`). A higher CC threshold makes that gap matter more.

## Selling 45–60 DTE costs more than it earns here

The evidence on entry DTE points toward short tenors, not away from them. AQR's Israelov and Tummala found front-month, near-the-money-to-moderately-OTM index options the most compensated to sell per unit of stress loss ([AQR](https://www.aqr.com/Insights/Research/Working-Paper/Which-Index-Options-Should-You-Sell)). Tosi and Ziegler found short-put returns concentrated in the final days. Selling one week out and holding gave a **Sharpe of 2.8 versus 0.66 for four weeks**, and selling two months out while holding one month gave 0.06 ([CXO summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/short-selling/when-to-sell-equity-index-put-options/)). Dew-Becker et al. found only short-maturity variance risk priced ([NBER w21182](https://www.nber.org/papers/w21182)). Andries et al. extend the significant premium out to roughly 6–9 months, but it decreases with maturity ([NY Fed SR 736](https://www.newyorkfed.org/medialibrary/media/research/staff_reports/sr736.pdf)). The opposite extreme does not dominate either. Cboe's monthly PUT index compounded **6.6% against 5.6% for weekly WPUT** over 2006–2015, gross, though weekly had a smaller maximum drawdown (−24% vs −33%) ([CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/equity-options/performance-of-cboe-putwrite-indexes)). tastylive's "45 DTE is optimal" comes from comparisons against 150 and 365 DTE, and from a 30-vs-60 tie. It is not evidence that 45 beats 7–21 ([tastylive](https://tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/optimal-dte-for-daily-income-01-20-2017); [tastylive](https://www.tastylive.com/shows/market-measures/episodes/comparing-30-and-60-dte-07-22-2016)). The team's model agrees in direction: edge per unit collateral falls from about 6.4% a year at 30 DTE to about 3.5% at 90 (model output). All of the rigorous evidence is on S&P 500 options, where the VRP is larger and more reliable than on single stocks.

Longer DTE does buy real things. Each position gets fewer management touches and fewer spread crossings a year. Percentage-of-IV spreads are tighter at longer maturities ([Hsieh & Jarrow](https://pubsonline.informs.org/doi/fpi/10.1287/mnsc.2017.2867)). A tested position keeps more extrinsic value to roll against. The system's constraints, though, turn these benefits into costs:

- **Earnings blackout.** It rejects any candidate whose expiry reaches the next earnings date (`src/engine/risk_engine.py:226-230`). With quarterly reports, the share of single-stock entries blocked rises from ~31% at 28 DTE to ~49% at 45 and ~66% at 60 (team estimate, DTE/91). A long-dated program would quietly become an ETF program.
- **Scoring and dedupe.** Ranking has no theta, yield or DTE term, and dedupes to one contract per underlying on liquidity and lower delta (`src/engine/scoring.py:37-55`, `src/engine/decision_engine.py:57-66`). Simply raising `dte_max` to 60 would mostly lose to front weeklies, which is why 16 of 21 fills landed at 7–10 DTE.
- **Fetch cost.** The chain fetch takes every expiration in the window (`src/ibkr/market_data.py:1078-1097`). A 7–60 window roughly doubles the number of expirations and widens IV-scaled strike bands by ×1.46. That is ~2–3× the contracts per symbol, and the team estimates it would push cold first sweeps from ~125 s to ~250 s, past the 150 s per-symbol timeout. It would also halve the symbols reached per 350 s cycle (system note, estimate from measured logs).
- **Line budget.** Peak lines stay at the 40-line batch size, but 40 wheel + 30 spreads + 20 monitor = 90 = the cap, enforced at load (`src/common/config.py:1177-1183`). Batches cannot grow to absorb more contracts, so the extra fetch cost lands entirely on wall-clock time.
- **Sizing.** Risk units scale with √DTE (`src/engine/capital.py:35-46`), so 60-DTE contracts consume ×1.46 the concentration budget of 28-DTE ones and stay open longer.

The cheap way to get the one benefit worth having is to decouple roll room from entry DTE. Rolls today must land inside the same 7–28 window (`src/strategies/rolling.py:77-104`), so a 21-DTE roll can extend at most about a week. That is a tight space for the `max_debit` / `min_delta_reduction` bounds. A separate roll-target window costs a per-position fetch, not a universe-wide one. If a longer-dated sleeve is still worth testing, the right form is an **ETF-only 30–45 DTE sleeve with expiration targeting**: keep the one or two expirations nearest a target DTE instead of every expiration in the window. Fetch cost then stays at or below today's, as the system note estimates.

## Twelve closed trades cannot settle this

The database holds **21 paper SELL fills and 12 buy-to-closes**, nearly all at ≤16 DTE. Its single `CLOSED_EARLY` outcome does not distinguish a profit-take from a loss exit, a manual close or a roll (`src/reporting/legs.py:83-145`). The one loss, GOOGL 335P closed at 2.35× its credit, is stored with the detail string `"buy-to-close (profit-take)"`, because the close path hard-codes that label (`src/execution/position_manager.py:109`). A back-of-envelope estimate (the writer's own, assuming per-trade P&L dispersion near 1× the credit and a true difference of ~8% of credit between thresholds, roughly the model's 50%-vs-80% gap) puts the sample needed to separate two thresholds live at **well over a thousand closed trades per arm**. At the current pace that is years. Live A/B testing is therefore not the arbiter. The decision has to rest on measured costs plus a backtest, with live data serving as a sanity check. Any change stays human-edited config under the eval fence; nothing in the ledger may move it automatically.

## Conclusion

The question assumed DTE is the variable that should drive the exit, but the evidence points elsewhere. Between 50% and 90% the threshold is nearly irrelevant to expected profit. The things that actually differ are friction per close, which is largest on small short-dated premiums, and whether freed capital finds a new home. The DTE-shaped intuition survives only as a risk-shaping rule for long-dated trades, where a time trigger near 21 DTE does the work and the percentage target can be generous. For a book that lives at 7–10 DTE, that makes the user's proposed direction roughly backwards: short-dated CSPs and covered calls are where a target above 50% is most likely to pay.

The more consequential finding is about the system's ability to learn rather than about any threshold. Today it cannot tell a winning close from a losing one in its own records, and it has never measured what a close costs. Until it can, every threshold argument, including this one, rests on index-option backtests and a model with constant implied volatility. Closing that measurement gap is cheap, does not touch the Rules Engine or the eval fence, and is the step that turns this from a borrowed rule of thumb into a decision grounded in the account's own fills.
